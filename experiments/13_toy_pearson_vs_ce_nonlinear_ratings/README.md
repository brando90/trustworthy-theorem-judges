# Experiment 13: toy, a flexible judge fitted by Pearson vs by cross-entropy on nonlinear synthetic ratings

**Doc link:** <https://github.com/brando90/trustworthy-theorem-judges/blob/main/experiments/13_toy_pearson_vs_ce_nonlinear_ratings/README.md>

**TLDR:** A synthetic toy where the human rating distribution over 0 to 5 depends nonlinearly on a task feature, with spread and disagreement varying while the mean is nearly flat (scenario A) or a monotone nonlinear function (scenario B). The same flexible judge is fitted by maximum Pearson (②), by cross-entropy (④) and by RPS, on 4 to 2,048 training tasks, 100 seeds each, and scored on fresh tasks against the true distribution (RPS first). **A toy that works by construction: an illustration of the argument, not evidence about the real ratings.** Numbers, power-law fits and the number of tasks needed: [results.md](results.md) (key findings first; they are hand-written, the tables are generated by make_report.py). Figures: [fig_excess_vs_n.png](fig_excess_vs_n.png), [fig_distributions.png](fig_distributions.png). Run log: [agent_log.md](agent_log.md).

## Why

Brando's argument (voice notes 11-10-2026): correlation does not converge to the human distribution and cross-entropy does. Pearson sees one number per task (the predicted mean) and ignores spread, calibration and disagreement, so even with unlimited data a Pearson-fitted judge has a gap to the true distribution that does not close. A proper score is minimized at the truth, so a CE-fitted judge's gap closes as data grows. Experiment 12 (real ratings, five cached judges) found a tie on RPS; this toy shows what the argument predicts when the judge class is rich enough and the data are not the limit. It extends experiment 02's synthetic selection test and the toy table, it does not replace them.

## What was run

- [scripts/toy.py](scripts/toy.py): data generator, the four fits (analytic gradients, L-BFGS), metrics, the n-to-infinity limit. [scripts/run_sweep.py](scripts/run_sweep.py): 2 scenarios x 11 values of n x 100 seeds, k = 3 raters per task. [scripts/make_report.py](scripts/make_report.py): tables, power-law fits, figures.
- Truth: beta-binomial on 0..5, mean mu(x), concentration kappa(x) = exp(1.5 + 2.2 cos(1.5 pi x)). Scenario A: mu = 0.5 + 0.08x (mean 2.1 to 2.9). Scenario B: mu = 0.1 + 0.8((x+1)/2)^2.
- Judge class (all fits): softmax of a linear map of 13 cosine/sine features of x. Ridge c/n with c = 1, same for all. All fits start at the training-marginal forecast plus a seeded perturbation of scale 0.01.
- "Pearson + head": the Pearson-fit judge's mean is the score; an ordinal head (softmax of intercept + slope x score, per rating) is then fitted by CE on the same training tasks.
- Test: 500 fresh tasks per seed against the true distribution. Excess RPS = mean over thresholds of (Qc - Pc)^2; excess CE = KL(true || judge), CE floor 1e-6.
- Power law: excess(n) = E + A n^(-alpha) fitted to the seed means for n >= 16; seed-bootstrap intervals; n needed for 0.01 RPS, 0.003 RPS and 0.05 CE.

## Limits

Toy by construction; the truth is smooth and one-dimensional, the judge class contains it up to a small approximation floor (visible in the n-to-infinity row of the CE fit), and k = 3 raters. The raw Pearson fit's output shape beyond its mean depends on the start and the ridge, so its numbers measure that choice as much as the objective; the head variant is the fairer "score, then calibrate" version. The power law is an empirical fit, not a bound. Excess RPS values are small in absolute terms, so the 0.01 RPS target is weak (see results.md). The real ratings (25 tasks, 75 artifacts, 195 expert ratings) are too few to extrapolate a curve like this with confidence. Misspecification, a second feature for the human mean, and varying raters per task were not tried.
